# Block 2, 04: first-hand studies of blind / low vision / print-disabled students in higher ed

accessed 2026-11-11

Rough notes. Everything below comes from the abstract, ERIC record, or page I actually opened. Where a page gave no detail I say "not stated". ERIC records were read through the ERIC API (api.ies.ed.gov/eric), which returns the same abstract as the eric.ed.gov record page. Several publisher pages (JVIB/JBIR on nfb.org, ACM DL, MSU repository, Birmingham repository) returned 403 or unreadable PDFs, so those were not used as sources.

### Access to Academic Materials for Post-Secondary Students with Print Disabilities (2005, NEADS final report)
https://neads.ca/en/about/projects/atam/executive_summary.php (student profile page: https://neads.ca/en/about/projects/atam/student_profiles.php)
method and sample: two surveys in fall 2004, one of students with print disabilities across Canada (130 student respondents), one of disability service providers (67). Of the 130 students, 36 (21%) were blind or visually impaired, 81 (47%) had a learning disability. Mostly Ontario, Alberta, Quebec, BC. 73 at universities, 35 at community colleges.
difficulties found: executive summary page does not report student findings, only background (about 3% of the world's literature is in alternate formats, materials delayed while institutions or provincial libraries produce them). Student profile page gives no difficulty list. (A web search snippet said the report found students do not get materials on time and that diagrams and special symbols in PDF and PowerPoint were not accessible to screen readers or OCR; I did not see that on the pages I opened, so treat as unverified.)
causes found: page lists cost, technology, copyright legislation, societal ignorance, low campus awareness, funding and service delivery limits, no centralized source. This is general background on the page, not tied explicitly to the student survey.
consequences found: inconsistent quality, delays, student confusion about where to get resources (as stated on the page).
workarounds found: students are often asked to produce their own alternate format materials, described as time consuming and exhausting. Many students mentioned Kurzweil to scan printed material. Most-used supports: academic accommodations, adaptive technology, alternate formats, tutors. Required formats: e-text, audio (analogue, digital), MP3, large print. 74 of 130 got financial aid; funding figures on the page are inconsistent (37.66% "sufficient", base unclear).
notes: only Canadian national student-side survey found. Old (2004 data). Good for the NEADS angle. Percentages on the profile page have an unclear base, so quote carefully.

### A narrative inquiry into the experiences of university students with visual impairments (2016, MA thesis, University of Calgary; Ostrowski)
https://ucalgary.ca/news/conversations-accessible-education (university news piece describing the thesis; I did not open the thesis itself)
method and sample: narrative inquiry, interviews with post-secondary students with visual impairments. Number of participants not stated on the page. Some had lost vision later in life.
difficulties found: accommodations on paper did not mean full participation. Course design and resources (textbooks, research articles) incompatible with students' tools. Accessibility handled reactively not proactively.
causes found: instructors with little knowledge of vision loss, instructor and institutional attitudes, assumptions about ability even when well meant.
consequences found: some students got no appropriate support, some had accommodations ignored. Examples: enlarged text given when the student needed good lighting; a student could not follow lectures when lights were dimmed for slides.
workarounds found: mainstream devices with built-in accessibility (discreet, convenient), specialized tools like text-to-speech, self-advocacy with instructors or accessibility services, working out solutions together with the instructor.
notes: Canadian. Secondary source (news piece), so details are thin. Related JVIB 2016 article by the same author on accommodations in Canada exists (seen only as a citation in search, not opened).

### "Everyone Is Normal, and Everyone Has a Disability": Narratives of University Students with Visual Impairment (2018, Social Inclusion, Almog)
https://cogitatiopress.com/socialinclusion/article/view/1697
method and sample: longitudinal qualitative study (grounded theory plus emancipatory disability research). 16 legally blind students at four Israeli universities, each interviewed over 2 years.
difficulties found: academic and social barriers in university life (page does not list specific material or lecture barriers).
causes found: not detailed on the page beyond social and structural barriers.
consequences found: students had to hold two identities, disabled person and student, and chose when and where to show each, weighing costs and benefits.
workarounds found: strategic self-presentation and adaptation strategies built by students.
notes: more about identity and disclosure than course material. Useful for why students may not ask for help.

### The experiences of young people with vision impairment making the transition into Higher Education (2020, PhD thesis, University of Birmingham; Hewett)
https://etheses.bham.ac.uk/id/eprint/10219
method and sample: longitudinal qualitative study, semi-structured interviews plus case study work. 39 young people with vision impairment applying to courses across England, Scotland, Wales.
difficulties found: abstract frames three areas: support on arriving at HE, how inclusive the environment was, and readiness for independent study. No concrete difficulty list on the page.
causes found: not stated on the abstract page.
consequences found: not stated.
workarounds found: thesis proposes a "Balanced Model" of support: inclusive institutional practice, individual adjustments, and student agency, built through gradual mutual accommodation.
notes: big sample. The page is only the abstract, so use it for design/sample, not specifics. Related papers by Hewett et al. (Educational Review 2021, EJSNE 2017) seen only in search results, not opened.

### Experiences of Students with Visual Impairments in Higher Education: Barriers and Facilitators (2021, British Journal of Special Education; Firat)
https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1311659
method and sample: semi-structured interviews with 6 students with visual impairments and 8 academics who teach them. Content analysis. (Turkey, per other sources; not on the abstract.)
difficulties found: lack of materials, difficulty getting lecture notes, weak academic support, problems with readers, insufficient library resources, access to and within the university.
causes found: academics showed limited awareness of these barriers; support available was insufficient.
consequences found: abstract does not say beyond "considerable barriers".
workarounds found: facilitators were mainly personal qualities, plus peer and academic support.
notes: directly about lecture notes and materials. Includes staff view, so can compare.

### Experiences of Visually Impaired Students in Community College Math Courses (2017, dissertation; Swan)
https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED577960
method and sample: personal interviews with 6 blind or visually impaired students at three community colleges in one Mid-Atlantic US state; 5 in developmental math, 1 in college-level math. Piaget's constructivism as framework.
difficulties found: abstract says math is hard for these students, attributed in cited research to the visual and interactive nature of the subject and how it is taught. No student-reported difficulties listed in the abstract.
causes found: visual nature of math and teaching style (from the cited research, not the findings).
consequences found: not stated.
workarounds found: what students said they need: motivating instructors, tutoring, assistive technology, a positive inclusive environment.
notes: closest match to math access from the student side. Abstract only, so the specific math notation or graph problems are not confirmed.

### Accessibility Challenges of Online Learning Affecting Successful Degree Completion among Visually Impaired Undergraduate/Graduate Students: An Exploratory Case Study (2022, dissertation; Riley-Ancar)
https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED648895
method and sample: exploratory case study, Zoom interviews with 10 visually impaired students taking at least one online course at 2-year or 4-year institutions.
difficulties found: five themes: incompatibility with assistive technology and websites; poor communication with professors; problems with the accommodations process; need for disability awareness training; difficulty accessing online campus services.
causes found: students tied the challenges to instructional design and delivery methods (and to their own learning styles).
consequences found: framed as challenges to completing degrees; no outcomes numbers on abstract.
workarounds found: not stated. Author suggests disability services, professors and instructional designers re-check virtual classroom accessibility policies.
notes: online/LMS side, relevant to course material delivered through platforms.

### Digital Classrooms: A Multiple Case Study of Four Visually Impaired College Students' Challenges and Opportunities (2017, dissertation; Malone)
https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED579435
method and sample: multiple case study, interviews with 4 blind or visually impaired college students. Constant comparative analysis.
difficulties found: obstacles in accessing, completing and submitting assignments in digital classrooms (e-textbooks, laptops, electronic forms, hyperlinks).
causes found: tech change with less face to face contact can increase exclusion for students who rely on assistive technology (author's argument).
consequences found: exclusion risk (author framing).
workarounds found: themes were strong support systems, staff professional development, and student self-motivation and self-advocacy.
notes: tiny sample. Abstract only.

### Barriers and Enablers for Visually Impaired Students at a UK Higher Education Institution (2011, British Journal of Visual Impairment; Bishop and Rhind)
https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ938451
method and sample: semi-structured interviews with 9 visually impaired students at one UK institution, Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis.
difficulties found: four themes: student's own attitude; institutional provision (campus navigability, central services support, school-level support); external support (travel, financial); others' attitudes (parents, staff).
causes found: gaps in institutional provision and staff attitudes (as theme labels; no detail on abstract).
consequences found: not stated.
workarounds found: engagement with support, positive self-identity listed under the student's own attitude theme.
notes: older. Not material-specific.

### Exploring the Academic and Social Integration of Students Who Are Blind and Visually Impaired Attending a Saudi Arabian University (2022, dissertation; Ajaj)
https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED648664
method and sample: 18 semi-structured interviews at a public Saudi university: 14 BVI students and 4 administrators. Interviews in Arabic, translated.
difficulties found: inaccessible course materials, lectures, physical environments, social events. Male students faced more barriers than female students (centres offered services to both).
causes found: not detailed; administrators knew of the barriers and were working on them.
consequences found: abstract notes poor integration can hurt self-esteem, self-determination, belonging and lead to attrition (background statement, not a finding).
workarounds found: not stated. Recommends blindness awareness training and tools to assess services and accommodations.
notes: non-Canadian context, so treat transfer carefully.

### Barriers to Effective Learning for Students with Visual Impairments at an Inclusive-Education University in Nigeria (2024, British Journal of Special Education; Nwosu, Orizu, Obiozor, Apiti)
https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1454285
method and sample: qualitative descriptive design, 11 undergraduates with blindness (7 male, 4 female), purposive sampling, semi-structured interviews, inductive analysis.
difficulties found: challenges arising from peers, lecturers, the university and families.
causes found: different contexts (ecological systems) shape learning outcomes; no specifics on abstract.
consequences found: affects learning outcomes (stated generally).
workarounds found: not stated.
notes: abstract is thin. Low priority.

### The Impact of Transition Services in Facilitating College Degree Completion for Students with Visual Impairments: Post-Bachelor's Degree Perspectives (2018, Journal of Postsecondary Education and Disability; Mask and DePountis)
https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1182379
method and sample: standardized open-ended interviews with 2 functionally blind graduates who completed a bachelor's degree.
difficulties found: themes: access to general curriculum and expanded core curriculum, career awareness, support systems, self-advocacy and self-determination, independent living and social skills, internships. Specific difficulties not on the abstract page.
causes found: gaps in transition services in high school and college (author argument for evidence-based transition plans).
consequences found: not stated.
workarounds found: self-advocacy and support systems named as themes.
notes: JPED, but only n=2 and about transition. Useful for the self-advocacy point.

### TADA: Making Node-link Diagrams Accessible to Blind and Low-Vision People (2024, ACM CHI; Zhao, Nacenta, Sukhai, Somanath)
https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04502
method and sample: design informed by interviews with 15 participants about challenges and strategies for accessing diagrams; then a tablet system (touch, tones, speech) evaluated with 25 participants. Page does not say whether the 15 were students or give demographics.
difficulties found: node-link diagrams (taxonomies, mind maps, textbook networks) are hard to access for blind and low-vision people. Specific interview findings not on the arXiv abstract page.
causes found: not stated on page.
consequences found: not stated.
workarounds found: interview participants shared strategies, but the page does not list them. The tool itself is a proposed fix.
notes: diagrams angle, partly Canadian authors (one at a Canadian accessibility organization per the author list, not verified further). Mostly a system paper, so use cautiously as student evidence.

## patterns

- Materials arrive late, in the wrong form, or incompatible with the student's tools (Firat lecture notes and library; NEADS delays and self-made formats; Ostrowski incompatible textbooks and articles; Riley-Ancar incompatible websites and assistive tech; Ajaj inaccessible course materials).
- Having an accommodation on paper is not the same as getting access: accommodations ignored or mismatched (Ostrowski), problems in the accommodations process (Riley-Ancar), support insufficient (Firat).
- Instructor awareness comes up again and again as the cause: limited awareness or training (Firat, Ostrowski, Malone, Riley-Ancar, Ajaj). Accessibility is dealt with reactively.
- Students carry the workload: making their own formats (NEADS), self-advocating (Ostrowski, Malone, Mask and DePountis), managing when to disclose (Almog), using mainstream devices (Ostrowski).
- Support students say they need is human and ongoing: supportive instructors, tutoring, peer support, plus assistive tech (Swan, Firat, Malone).
- Visual content (math, diagrams, slides) is named as a problem, but student-side detail is thin in the abstracts I could open (Swan, TADA, NEADS snippet unverified).

## goes against

- Riley-Ancar and Ajaj report administrators or services were aware and working on it (Ajaj: administrators knew of the barriers), so not every institution is ignoring the issue.
- Bishop and Rhind and Firat list the student's own attitude and personal qualities as main facilitators, so access is not only about institutions failing; some students cope well.
- Firat found peer and academic support as facilitators, and Ajaj found centres offering services, so services can help when they exist.
- Several STEM access papers found while searching (chemistry, physics, biology labs, e.g. ERIC EJ1203690, EJ1452276, EJ1003027) are accounts written by instructors or the student/instructor team about one adapted course, not systematic student interviews. They show fixes can work with preparation (e.g. printing Braille materials ahead of the semester) but are not evidence of typical experience.
- Not found: a Journal of Postsecondary Education and Disability study focused on blind students in STEM; the JPED STEM interview study I found (Friedensen et al., 2021, 16 interviews) covers students with non-apparent high-incidence disabilities, so it was left out.
- Gap: no open abstract with first-hand detail on slide decks or diagrams in lecture from blind students in Canada beyond NEADS (2005).

## summary
- 12 student-side qualitative or survey studies plus one diagram study, 2005 to 2024, found and opened (mostly abstract level).
- Late or incompatible materials, accommodations not matching real needs, and instructor unawareness repeat across countries; students self-advocate and build workarounds.
- Weak spots: little concrete math, diagram, and slide detail from students, only one Canadian survey (NEADS 2005), and several abstracts lacked findings detail.
