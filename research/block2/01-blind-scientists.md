### Wanda Diaz-Merced (EarthSky profile)
https://earthsky.org/space/space-data-into-sound-patterns-wanda-diaz-merced/
type: profile
who: astronomer, lost sight progressively (diabetic retinopathy), fully blind by 29 per the article
goal: keep studying math/physics then astronomy
difficulty: blind spots began while studying math and physics around 19; plots and data are visual
cause: vision loss, plus data presented as plots
consequence: article calls those difficult years; she resolved to keep going

### Mona Minkara (Chemistry World)
https://www.chemistryworld.com/careers/how-pragmatic-is-it-to-be-a-blind-scientist/3007425.article
type: interview / profile
who: blind computational chemist, now bioengineering professor
goal: undergrad chemistry (Wellesley), PhD, then teaching and research
difficulty: teachers resisted advanced science courses; wet labs needed readers/helpers; could not watch protein dynamics videos
cause: practicality-driven resistance, visual content
consequence: needed people alongside her in labs; support varied by university (said Florida "laid down the red carpet")
workaround: plotted the dynamics mathematically and found patterns; assistants built protein models from Play-Doh; team of undergraduate access assistants ("Mink Inc") take notes, read materials, describe plots
notes: "People were not resisting out of malice, they were just resisting out of practicality." Good source on human access assistants as a long-term workaround.

### Minkara team paper on blind STEM educators (PMC)
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8169403
type: paper written by her team (mostly third person; includes assistant quotes)
who: Minkara plus access assistants
goal: teach an equation and figure heavy bioengineering course
difficulty: equations/figures; she does not read braille fast enough for quick reference; Word/Canvas documents that passed accessibility checkers were not truly accessible to blind user; Word had no alt text for equations
cause: braille speed, tools that check boxes but fail real use
consequence: reading equations aloud caused errors; remote teaching slower (describing a graph's shape took up to 15 min online vs about 1 min in person)
workaround: memorizing lectures read aloud; fill-in-the-blank outlines; agreed spoken terms for equations; saving each equation as an image with alt text
notes: Assistant quote: "I think the key word is 'known'." Directly relevant to slides, PDFs, math. Teaching side, not student side, but same material problems.

### Abraham Nemeth (1991 interview, Raised Dot Computing newsletter)
https://www.duxburysystems.org/downloads/library/texas/nemeth/abe1991.htm
type: interview
who: blind mathematician, creator of Nemeth Braille code
goal: study math in college, take notes
difficulty: counselors said a blind person could not have a math career; existing braille math code (Taylor) clumsy
cause: attitudes, poor notation
consequence: majored in psychology first; later PhD in math at Wayne State
workaround: sighted readers read math aloud with rules he invented; he turned those rules into a braille code for note-taking
notes: "I heard this from so many counselors that I believed it." "I began working on a braille code which simulated my rules for speech." Wife encouraged math. Historic (older era) but same themes: readers plus notation.

### Aqil Sajjad, John Gardner, Michael Whapples, Daniel Hajas (Physics World feature)
https://physicsworld.com/?p=66753
type: magazine feature on blind physicists (several people, paraphrase plus quotes)
who: blind physicists/students
goal: study and do physics
difficulty: Sajjad: told he could not be a scientist, mathematical braille too slow, publishers would not share LaTeX of technical books (copyright). Gardner: fitting models to experimental data needs "the eyes of other people"; a funding panel withdrew support doubting a blind person could do physics. Whapples: lecturers unfamiliar with braille, math braille conversion by staff without science background gave inconsistent results
cause: copyright, staff training, attitudes, visual graphs
consequence: funding withdrawn (Gardner); inconsistent materials (Whapples)
workaround: Triangle (LaTeX-like text-to-speech for equations); mother read to Sajjad; Harvard grad student wrote LaTeX converter; audio-tactile graphics (ViewPlus); note-taking assistant; Hajas built tactile display prototype and an image description site with sighted volunteers
notes: Strong source for math and graphics. Article is secondary reporting, so quote sparingly. Gardner argues prejudice is bigger than technology now (per search snippet; not confirmed on page text I saw).

## patterns
- Reading and notes depend on other people: readers, access assistants, friends explaining lectures (Nemeth, Cullers, Sajjad, Minkara, Yeo, Wedler asking for reading help).
- Math and equations are the hardest content: braille math was slow or inconsistently produced; people used spoken rules, Nemeth/braille, or LaTeX-to-speech; copyright blocked getting LaTeX of books (Sajjad).
- Graphs, plots, diagrams and board work are visual blockers; fixes were hand tracing, sonification, 3D models, tactile graphics, friends drawing on the hand (Minkara, Diaz-Merced, Cullers, Gardner, Wedler).
- Materials only work if prepared ahead: lessons sent in advance (Yeo), braille conversion by non-science staff gave errors (Whapples), "accessible" documents that passed checkers still failed (Minkara team).
- Attitudes of advisors and teachers repeatedly blocked or delayed STEM paths (Wedler, Nemeth, Minkara, Sajjad, Gardner funding), sometimes more than materials.
- Role models and mentors (blind scientists, camps) helped people keep going (Supalo, Wedler, Bower).

## goes against
- Vermeij: sources I opened say little about material access and he reportedly downplays blindness' effect; his field uses touch-based specimens, so his case may not transfer to slide/PDF-heavy courses.
- Gardner (per Physics World) says prejudice is now the main problem, not technology. Hajas argues modern physics with instrument readouts should suit blind people.
- Supalo gave no specific material barriers; "just problem solve".
- Several profiles are upbeat and short; they may leave out the bad parts.
- Survivorship bias: everyone here succeeded, often with strong family or institutional support. People who dropped out are not in these sources, so difficulty is probably understated. Also many are older accounts (Nemeth, Cullers), tools have changed. Several sources are journalist summaries, not the person's own words.

## summary
- Across 15 sources, the repeating problems are equations/graphs/board work and slow or inconsistent access to materials, handled mostly with human readers and assistants plus custom tools.
- Attitudes of teachers and advisors were as big a barrier as the materials in many accounts.
- Evidence is skewed to successful people and often secondary; the best first hand ones to read fully are Nemeth 1991, Cullers (Almanac), Wedler, and Minkara.
