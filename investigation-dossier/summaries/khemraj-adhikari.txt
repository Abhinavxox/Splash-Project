1. Software Engineering Management for Increased E-Commerce Security and to
Overcome E-Commerce Threats

Authors, year, where published: Mohammad Akbar, 2020, Cross Current International
Journal of Economics, Management and Media Studies, Vol. 2 Issue 2, pp. 42–44

Type of study: Review (short, 3 pages)

Who they studied: Nobody. It summarises earlier papers on e-commerce security and
trust.

Main ﬁnding: Privacy, integrity, conﬁdentiality and non-repudiation are the main
security dimensions for e-commerce. Firewalls, VPNs and anti-virus are the usual
protections, but the author says no single measure is enough. Encryption, digital
signatures, veriﬁcation and intrusion detection are also needed.

Engineer vs manager: Says almost nothing about either role. Despite "software
engineering management" in the title, the paper never discusses how managers or
engineers handle security.

Useful for us because: Only as background that security and customer trust are major
barriers. It has no evidence of its own.

Quote or number worth citing: A Trojan horse lets an attacker see cleartext before it is
encrypted, which defeats password protection and encryption (p. 43). No numbers.

Warning: Weak source. The header says Issue 2 but the page footer says 2(1). The text
cites "Godwin (2001)" but the reference list has Udo (2001).

2. Software Testing and Release Decision at Di erent Statistical Conﬁdence Levels
with Consideration of Debuggers' Learning and Negligent Factors

Authors, year, where published: Chun-Wu Yeh and Chih-Chiang Fang, 2024,
International Journal of Industrial Engineering Computations, Vol. 15, pp. 105–126

Type of study: Mathematical modelling (a software reliability growth model), tested on
8 published datasets plus a made-up example company

Who they studied: No people. They used 8 historical failure datasets from earlier
studies (e.g. a telecom system, Mozilla Firefox 3.5 testing data, an open-source project
management tool).

Main ﬁnding: Their model adds a learning factor (testers get better at spotting known
error patterns) and a negligent factor (debuggers introduce new errors while ﬁxing). It
ﬁts both S-shaped and exponential data and ranked in the top two of four models on
most datasets. They also propose conﬁdence intervals that narrow over time, so
managers can plan for a worst case.
Engineer vs manager: Both, indirectly. The learning and negligence factors describe
engineers' (testers'/debuggers') behaviour. The release-time decision model is a tool for
managers.

Useful for us because: It links engineer skill and training directly to manager decisions
on cost and release timing. The authors say improving learning and detection needs
more training or senior sta , which costs money, so managers face a trade-o .

Quote or number worth citing: In the example, the best plan (P2) gives an optimal
release at 3.45 months for $125,441, reaching 95.38% reliability after the warranty. A
cautious worst-case view pushes release to 3.7 months (p. 121).

3. Factors A ecting the Adoption of Cloud for Software Development: A Case from
Turkey

Authors, year, where published: Erhan Pisirir, Oumout Chouseinoglou, Cüneyt Sevgi
and Erkan Uçar, 2023, International Journal of Information Technology & Decision
Making (World Scientiﬁc), DOI 10.1142/S0219622023500517

Type of study: Survey (personally administered questionnaire), analysed with Structural
Equation Modeling (SEM)

Who they studied: 191 developers, project managers and senior managers from 30
software organisations in Turkey, covering 84 projects. This gave 268 valid responses
after cleaning. 83.2% of respondents were male.

Main ﬁnding: The model combines TAM, TOE and the authors' new Personal–
Organization–Project (POP) factors, and explains 69.6% of intention to use the cloud. 15
of 21 hypotheses were accepted. Perceived suitability is the strongest driver of
intention, and top management support is the strongest driver of actual use. Actual
cloud use in Turkey is still low.

Engineer vs manager: Directly compares both. Developers ﬁnd the cloud more suitable
for larger projects and projects with more licensed software, but management is less
likely to migrate. The authors suggest a sunk-cost e ect: managers who bought
expensive licences resist switching.

Useful for us because: It's the strongest source here. It has a real sample, includes
both roles, and shows that what developers want depends on what managers support.

Quote or number worth citing: Top management support → actual use, standardised
weight 0.636 (p. 2350051-21). Perceived suitability → intention, 0.644 (p. 2350051-18).

4. Project Risk Management in Iranian Software Projects
Authors, year, where published: Ali Reza Afshari, Vladimir Brtka and Melita Ćoćkalo-
Hronjec, 2018, Journal of Engineering Management and Competitiveness (JEMC), Vol. 8
No. 2, pp. 81–88

Type of study: Survey (structured questionnaire, done as interviews)

Who they studied: 30 software project managers or IT managers in Iran (a planned
sample of 50 dropped to 30). When a manager was too busy, a senior developer
answered instead.

Main ﬁnding: Risk assessment is poorly practised. Most managers deal with risks only
as they appear, don't document them, and don't reuse past experience. The authors
propose a simple risk assessment framework and tool.

Engineer vs manager: Mostly the manager view. But many of the risks managers listed
are about engineers: over-reliance on a few skilled developers, high sta turnover and
sta lacking the right skills.

Useful for us because: It's a manager-only source, like Paper 6 in Sirjana's notes, and
shows managers blaming user and sta ing issues for delays.

Quote or number worth citing: 83% of managers assess risk only implicitly and use an
ad hoc approach; 93% have never used a risk tool (pp. 86–87).

Warning: Small, purposive sample from one country, so results are weak evidence.

5. RAG-Based AI Agents for Enterprise Software Development: Implementation
Patterns and Production Deployment

Authors, year, where published: Xiuyuan Zhao, Tiejiang Sun, Shaochen Ren, Jingyun
Yang and Yang Liu, 2025, Frontiers in Artiﬁcial Intelligence Research, Vol. 2 Issue 3, pp.
501–520

Type of study: Review

Who they studied: Nobody. It's a technical overview of AI agents that use retrieval-
augmented generation (RAG).

Main ﬁnding: RAG agents can help with code completion, test generation,
documentation and code review. Deploying them in companies brings problems with
speed, cost, security, hallucinations and evaluation.

Engineer vs manager: Mostly about developers as users. The "organizational change
management" section touches on managers: training, pilot programs, team champions
and developers' fears about job loss.

Useful for us because: Background on how AI may change developers' work, similar to
Paper 5 in Sirjana's notes. No evidence about real engineers or managers.
Quote or number worth citing: Nothing reliable.

Warning: Weak source. Figure captions read like unﬁnished instructions ("Diagram
should illustrate…"), the reference list has a stray sentence, and some references are
o -topic (tall buildings, tax fraud). Candidate for replacement.

Conclusions

      Engineers and managers together: Paper 3 only. Paper 2 models engineer
       behaviour inside a manager's decision tool.

      Managers only: Paper 4.

      Nobody studied: Papers 1 and 5 (reviews).

      Strongest sources: Paper 3, then Paper 2 and Paper 4.

      Weak sources: Papers 1 and 5 are low-tier and candidates for "no" and a
       replacement. Paper 4 could be kept as weak evidence.

      Key references to look up: Schmidt, Lyytinen, Keil and Cule (2001), an
       international Delphi study of software project risks (from Paper 4); Tiwana and
       Keil (2004), the one-minute risk assessment tool (Paper 4); Butt et al. (2019) on
       cloud for agile development (Paper 3); Chiu, Huang and Lee (2008) on learning
       e ects in testing (Paper 2).

Interview ideas

      Paper 3 says management support matters most for actually using new tools. Do
       engineers feel blocked by managers, and do managers see it that way?

      Paper 3 suggests managers stick with paid tools because of sunk cost. Do
       professionals recognise this?

      Paper 4 says managers handle risks only when they appear. Do engineers see
       risks earlier than managers do?

      Paper 2 says training improves testers but costs money. Who should decide how
       much to invest: engineers or managers?

      Paper 5 mentions developers fearing job loss to AI. Do managers and engineers
       see AI tools di erently?
