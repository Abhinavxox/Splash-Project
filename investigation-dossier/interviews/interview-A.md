# Interview A

Interviewer: Nishan Giri
Participant: Professional A, software engineer with more than 3 years of industry experience (about two of them at the current company), B.Eng. Name and company are kept out of this repository and go only in the confidential accompanying document for the instructors.
Format: spoken interview, transcribed.

---

**Professional A:** It's a pleasure talking with you. To introduce myself, I work as a Full Stack Software Engineer with [company], and I've been working there for two years now.

**Q1. In agile teams, most people often say engineers decide how the work gets done and the manager just clears blockers. Do you think that's really how it works in most teams?**

In my experience, that's generally the idea, but the reality is a little bit more collaborative, I think. Engineers usually understand the technical details best, right? So they are in the best position to evaluate different implementation approaches, also technical feasibility, effort, and also the estimates. But that doesn't mean that they make every decision independently.

For example, a product manager might explain a feature that customers need, and the engineering team might identify two ways to build it. One approach could take like two weeks, while another could deliver most of the value in like three days. The engineers can explain the tradeoffs, and the product manager can help decide which option makes more sense based on business priorities.

A team lead has to bridge those two perspectives by supporting technical decisions and understanding the team's capabilities and helping remove blockers. I would say engineers should have autonomy over technical decisions, but the best decisions happen when engineers and managers work together.

**Q2. How do managers usually decide if a developer is doing a good job? Would engineers judge it the same way?**

I think it depends on the organization and the manager, but generally performance is judged on several things, like delivery, quality of work, problem solving, communication, and also collaboration.

From a manager's perspective, they might look at whether someone delivers reliably and contributes to team goals, and also communicates risk early. While engineers might look more closely at technical quality, maintainability, and how well someone handles difficult problems, and whether their solutions prevent future issues.

For example, a developer might finish a feature quickly, but if the implementation creates technical debt or recurring bugs, that might not be a good indicator of overall performance. On the other hand, someone who takes a little longer because they uncovered an unexpected technical issue and solved it properly may be creating more long-term value.

I think both perspectives are important. Good performance isn't just about completing tasks on time, it's about delivering useful, reliable software and also working effectively with the team.

**Q3. How can managers keep track of progress without it feeling like micromanaging?**

I think it comes down to trust, clear expectations, and good communication. At the beginning of a task, the team should understand the requirements, the expected outcome, and the estimated effort. After that, managers should give engineers enough space to figure out the implementation. We have regular standups and progress updates, which can be useful because they help identify blockers early. But if a manager constantly asks for updates, or checks every small detail, then it can interrupt the developer's work and make them feel like they aren't trusted.

Another thing to remember is that estimates aren't always accurate, and sometimes we discover an unexpected dependency or technical problem that wasn't obvious at the beginning. In that situation, I would rather have an engineer communicate the issue early and discuss the revised estimate than feel pressure to deliver something unrealistic. I think managers should track outcomes, risks, and blockers rather than try to control every step of the development process.

**Q4. AI tools now help with coding and manager tasks like estimates and reports. Will AI change engineers' jobs more or managers' jobs more in the next few years?**

I think AI will change both roles significantly, in different ways. It's kind of difficult to say which role will be affected more. From an engineering perspective, AI can help with writing routine code, generating tests, explaining unfamiliar code, and suggesting ways to debug problems. That can make developers more productive and give them more time to focus on complex problems.

However, building reliable software involves more than just generating code. Engineers still need to understand the system, validate AI-generated solutions, make architecture decisions, consider security, and understand the impact on users.

As for managers, AI can help summarize meetings, prepare reports, organize information, and support planning and estimation. But prioritizing work still requires understanding customers, business strategy, team capabilities, and organizational context. My expectation is that AI will automate some repetitive tasks in both roles rather than completely replace either role. Engineers may spend less time writing routine code, while managers may spend less time gathering information and preparing reports.

Thank you.
