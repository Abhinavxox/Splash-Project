Professional B described a career of more than 25 years: engineer and senior engineer roles, about six years as a principal level engineer at a large software company, about fifteen years in engineering leadership on software for embedded and measurement systems, and now leading a small startup building an AI learning platform.] So I am happy to answer your questions and give my opinion based on my experience.

**Q1. In agile teams people often say engineers decide how the work gets done and the manager just clears blockers. Is that really how it works in most teams?**

See, in theory that is what agile says, but in practice I think it is team decisions most of the time. The work is done with collaboration and joint discussions. Senior engineers have a big say in the how, but the manager is not just removing blockers. A good manager gives the context: why we are building this, what is the priority, what the customer is expecting. At [a large company] it was more structured, we had design reviews, architects and all that. At [a smaller firm] the team was smaller, so everybody sat together, discussed and decided. So it is not so clean as engineers decide and manager clears blockers.

**Q2. In many companies the lead or manager talks to customers and passes priorities down to developers. Is that still common, or do developers talk to customers directly more now?**

I still think mostly the leads and managers talk to customers and pass the priorities to developers during sprint planning, but it depends on the company. At [a smaller firm], like I said, our customers were semiconductor companies, so many times our engineers had to go to the customer site for integration or debugging on the machine. And at [the startup], since we are small, we talk to the teachers and students directly. I think it is good if developers hear the customer sometimes, but not on every call, otherwise nobody gets any work done.

**Q3. Does a good engineer naturally make a good manager, and what changes when someone moves from engineering into management?**

No, I don't think so. A good engineer may not have the leadership experience or the skills. These are different skills. If they have them or develop them, then yes, they can be naturally good managers. What changes is your success is no longer your own work, it is the team output. You write much less code and you spend time on people, hiring, feedback, handling conflicts. I went through this myself when I moved into the leadership role. The hardest part honestly is not jumping in and fixing the code yourself.

**Q4. When speed and quality clash on a deadline, who usually pushes for which, and who makes the final call?**

Usually the business side pushes for speed: sales, product management, sometimes the manager. And engineers push for quality. Final call is generally the manager or product owner, with input from the engineering lead. But you have to be clear what quality you are talking about. Like in metrology, the accuracy of the measurement you cannot compromise. If a customer is measuring wafers and our number is wrong, that is a big problem. But some UI polish or an extra feature, that can wait. So my approach was always: cut the scope, not the quality. And whatever shortcut we take, we write it down as technical debt.

**Q5. How do managers usually judge whether a developer is doing well, and would senior engineers judge it the same way?**

Not exactly. Managers look at delivery: are commitments being met, are tickets getting closed, how is the communication. Senior engineers look at the quality of the work, the design, how they handle a difficult problem. And one thing I always looked at is: does this person make the work of others easier or harder? Some people close many tickets but leave a mess for others. So the best is when the manager takes input from the senior engineers also.

**Follow up (second interviewer). If the manager is not technical, can they see that quality part properly?**

It is difficult. Then they have to depend on the tech lead. That is why that relationship between manager and tech lead is so important.

**Q6. When an estimate turns out to be wrong, what usually happens in a team? Is raising it early rewarded or seen as a problem?**

Estimates are wrong all the time, that is normal in software. In a good team raising it early is appreciated. In a bad culture people hide it till the last day and then it is a surprise for everybody. I always told my team: tell me early, then I have options. I can reduce the scope, I can inform the customer. And that goes back to what I said about cutting scope. You can only do that if you know early.

**Q7. How are AI tools changing the work of senior engineers, and do you see managers using them too, for example for estimates or reports?**

It is changing a lot and very fast. At [the startup] we are building an AI product, so we use these tools every day. Code generation, test cases, documentation, all that is much faster now. What I see is the senior engineer is now spending more time reviewing and judging. The code comes very quickly, but it can look correct and still have a subtle bug. So that quality part I mentioned, what senior engineers look at, that becomes even more important now. Managers also, yes, for status summaries, reports, presentations. For estimates it can be a starting point, but it does not know your team or what happened last sprint. That judgment still has to come from people.

**Q8. If you could change one thing about how engineers and managers work together, what would it be?**

Involve the engineers earlier. Many times the manager or sales makes a commitment to the customer and then comes to engineering with a date. Then the estimate feels forced and the manager feels the team is slow. If engineers are there from the beginning, the commitment is more realistic and the team feels ownership. That is really the one thing.
